## Reasons, on merit

- 298 and 299 go first of the four. Every card relies on the trunk-based single-writer rule, and both close holes in it. The hand-run case had two recurrences on card 230 itself (a seat overwrote a committed proposal; two facilitator processes raced a push). 298 is the smaller change, with one lock and three checks, but it protects the vote result, so it can be argued either way. 299 follows 298 because its hook refuses writes while a vote lock is held, so it builds on the 298 fix and needs the lock to be correct first.
- 300 and 301 follow. They are card 297's sign-off conditions, so they finish its work: a legacy turn must not start a seat that cannot run commands, and the probe must not pass on text copied from its own prompt. They are not urgent for delivery. Codex build seats stay refused on this machine until the machine fix is approved, so no seat can reach either gap today. They should land before that fix is applied, because the fix is the point at which both gaps become reachable. 300 goes before 301 because it is the wider exposure: a seat that starts and cannot run a command wastes a whole turn, while 301 is a probe false-pass that needs a model to quote its prompt.
- All four sit ahead of the rename (196, 197, 266) because the rename is a long multi-card run of serial writes on one checkout, and the single-writer gaps are most likely to bite there.
- 264 stays first: it is the only card here that names an open audit FAIL (audit 123), and stop-the-line puts it ahead of everything. 217 stays second because every product green runs the acceptance suite it fixes.
